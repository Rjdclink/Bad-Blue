Live Two-Way Legal Conversation

Legal problems rarely fit into one sentence. LegalWhat lets you explain what happened naturally by voice or text, answer follow-up questions, add missing facts, and continue the discussion without starting over after every response.

Start a Legal Conversation

More than a one-shot legal search

A single search query can miss the facts that change the legal analysis. A two-way conversation allows the discussion to narrow the issue, separate important facts from side details, and identify what information is still missing.

Voice or text

Use whichever format is easier for the situation. You can speak naturally when you need to explain a complicated sequence of events, or type when exact names, dates, citations, or document language matter.

Follow-up questions have a purpose

Useful clarifying questions can establish the jurisdiction, timeline, parties, existing court orders, procedural posture, deadlines, or the exact result you are trying to understand. The goal is not more conversation for its own sake; it is a better factual foundation.

Conversation can lead into deeper tools

Once the issue is understood, the same matter can move into legal research, file analysis, public-record research, or legal document preparation instead of forcing you to re-explain the situation in a separate tool.

What a strong legal conversation should establish

  • The jurisdiction: the state, court, agency, or other authority that may control the issue.
  • The timeline: the order of important events, including notices, filings, hearings, payments, communications, and deadlines.
  • The procedural posture: whether the matter is only a dispute, already filed, awaiting a hearing, under appeal, or at another stage.
  • The source documents: the order, contract, notice, complaint, policy, correspondence, or evidence whose actual language matters.
  • The user's immediate goal: understand the problem, prepare for a deadline, research a rule, review a document, or create a draft.

Example of a two-way sequence

  1. You explain: “I was served with a civil complaint today and I do not know what to do.”
  2. The conversation narrows the issue by asking where the case was filed, the date of service, and whether the summons states a response period.
  3. You upload the complaint and summons so names, allegations, court information, and stated deadlines can be read from the source documents.
  4. You ask follow-up questions about the allegations, defenses to research, and what type of response may be required.
  5. If document preparation becomes appropriate, the same factual context can carry into a draft rather than requiring you to re-enter the entire case history.

When to use voice, text, or a file

Voice is useful for telling a complicated story or timeline naturally. Text is useful for exact names, dates, citations, numbers, and corrections. File upload is preferable when the answer depends on the wording of a court order, contract, notice, pleading, letter, or other document.

A productive conversation can move between all three. The important part is preserving the same matter context while using the input method best suited to the information being added.
