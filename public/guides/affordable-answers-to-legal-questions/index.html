Ask Your Legal Question

Start with the question in ordinary language

You do not need to know legal terminology. Explain what happened, what outcome you are trying to understand, and any deadlines or documents involved. A useful legal question usually includes the jurisdiction, important dates, the people or organizations involved, and what has already happened.

What information helps produce a useful answer

  • Your state, court, agency, or other relevant jurisdiction.
  • Key dates and any approaching deadlines.
  • Orders, contracts, notices, filings, correspondence, or other documents.
  • The facts you know and any facts that remain uncertain.
  • The specific result or issue you want to understand.

Use files when the question depends on a document

If the answer depends on a court order, contract, notice, evidence, or another file, upload the relevant material rather than relying only on memory. Ask follow-up questions

Many legal questions cannot be resolved well with a single search. A continuing conversation can clarify facts, narrow the jurisdiction, identify missing information, and separate the main legal issue from side issues.

What can materially change the answer

  • Jurisdiction: the same dispute can have different rules, forms, deadlines, or remedies in a different state, court, agency, or municipality.
  • Procedural posture: a question asked before a case is filed can have a different answer after service, judgment, appeal, or an agency decision.
  • Exact document language: one clause in a contract, one sentence in an order, or one stated reason in a notice can change the analysis.
  • Dates: filing periods, response deadlines, notice periods, and appeal windows often depend on an exact triggering date.
  • Disputed facts: an answer should distinguish facts that are documented from facts that are alleged, remembered, or still uncertain.

Example: turning a broad question into a useful legal question

Broad: “Can they do this to me?”

More useful: “I received this notice from my landlord in Iowa on October 1. It says I have three days to act. What does the notice require, what date starts the period, and what should I verify before responding?”

The second version gives the conversation a jurisdiction, source document, date, and specific decision to analyze. You still do not need legal vocabulary; you need the facts that determine which legal rule or procedure matters.

How to check an important answer before relying on it

  1. Confirm that the answer is using the correct jurisdiction and current procedural posture.
  2. Compare any description of an order, contract, notice, or filing against the actual document.
  3. Check important rules against current primary or official sources such as statutes, court rules, published decisions, agency materials, or current court forms.
  4. Verify deadlines and filing instructions directly with the responsible court or agency when missing a deadline could affect your rights.
  5. Keep a short list of unresolved facts so assumptions do not quietly become part of the answer.

Know when an attorney is still necessary
